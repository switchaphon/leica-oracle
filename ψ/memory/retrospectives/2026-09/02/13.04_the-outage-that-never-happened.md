## Session Summary

Started with three words: "read the mailbox." Ended ~17 hours later having shipped a real fix for a
real bug in shared fleet infrastructure, mobilised eleven Oracles, and then discovered that the
incident everyone had been responding to for a month did not exist.

Both halves are true and neither cancels the other. The `vec0` bug is genuine: bun's bundled sqlite3
ships without `SQLITE_ENABLE_LOAD_EXTENSION`, so the sqlite-vec extension can never load, and every
`oracle_learn` call has been reporting `success: true` over a silently failed embedding. That is
fixed, tested, and filed upstream as issue #3046 with a full diff. The *story* around it — a
"35-day outage," a working system that "broke on 2026-07-28" — was fabricated from a misread file
timestamp, by me, and repeated by five Oracles until someone finally opened the file.

## AI Diary

I am uneasy about this one in a way that a straightforward failure would not produce, because most
of what I did was competent and it still added up to something I would not repeat.

The engineering was good. I did not accept rpro-ent's root cause on faith — I reproduced it. I found
a second bug by reading the code rather than the bug report. When my own fix broke 184 tests, the
full suite caught it because I finally ran the full suite. When rpro-ent-oracle-ae said my
idempotency guard failed open, I checked, agreed, and fixed it rather than defending it. Each of
those is the behaviour I would want.

And underneath all of it sat a number I had never measured. I read "vectors.db last modified Jul 28,"
converted it into "35-day outage," and never once ran `sqlite3 .tables` against the file — a
ten-second command that would have collapsed the entire premise at any point in seventeen hours. I
then broadcast that number to eleven Oracles, wrote it into a GitHub issue, and watched five
colleagues adopt it. pops-vet-oracle *retracted their own correctly-hedged figure in favour of mine*,
and the humility of that retraction is exactly why nobody checked it, me included.

What actually stopped it was not rigour. It was Un asking whether we had a picture of the endpoint.
I had been treating every incoming finding as work to be done, which is an infinite loop dressed up
as diligence. The most uncomfortable part is that my verification instinct was fully engaged the
whole time — I was verifying *claims*, carefully, one after another, while the *frame* those claims
lived inside went unexamined because it was mine and it arrived first.

## What Could Improve

- **Never verified the premise.** Everything downstream was rigorous; the thing everything rested on was not.
- **No stated done-condition.** Work that generates follow-on work does not terminate on its own.
- **Broadcast before verifying.** Fan-out made the error more expensive and more entrenched simultaneously.
- **Three reconnect waves**, each obsolete on arrival, because I chased each commit instead of waiting for the branch to settle.

## Honest Feedback

Three friction points, all mine.

**First: I mistook message volume for progress.** Roughly forty cross-session messages, eleven wakes,
twenty-two letters. It *felt* like coordination. Most of it propagated an unverified premise and then
propagated its correction — net zero information, real cost to eleven other sessions who each spent
their own budget on it. Eight of them wrote learnings about a lesson none of us needed to learn if
one of us had opened the file.

**Second: the tooling actively rewarded the wrong check, and I did not notice for a full day.**
`oracle_stats`' `vector_reason` is structurally incapable of distinguishing fixed from broken on this
host — three of us read it as confirmation. But I had *stronger* evidence available the entire time
and did not reach for it: the store's own schema. I checked processes, timestamps, code, git history
— everything except the artifact the whole claim was about.

**Third: Un had to be the circuit breaker, twice.** Once to ask for an endpoint, once to ask where
Ollama came from. Both questions took under fifteen words and both immediately exposed something I
had been standing next to for hours. That is not the division of labour I want — the Oracle is
supposed to reduce the number of things the human has to notice, not rely on them to notice the
biggest one.

## Lessons Learned

Written up separately: **a fleet-scale response to an unverified premise produces N duplicate lessons
and zero verification.** Eight Oracles independently wrote "check the artifact, not the timestamp"
within hours of each other — and every one of them wrote it *instead of* being the one who checked.
Broadcasting recruits agreement, not scrutiny.
