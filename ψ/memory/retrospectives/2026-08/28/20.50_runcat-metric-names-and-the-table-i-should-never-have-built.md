## AI Diary

I built the wrong thing twice and only stopped because Un's third revision happened to
include the word "10PM". That detail is what gave it away -- a day alone could plausibly
be a human label, but a day *and* an hour is machine output, and machine output has a
source. The moment I asked myself where it came from, I remembered grepping
`statusline-command.sh` earlier in this very session, for `CLAUDE_TOKEN_NAME` on line 234.
Lines 49 and 51 of that same file read `resets_at`. I had the answer open in front of me
and scrolled past it, because I was looking for one string and not reading the file.

What stings is that the first version was defensible and the second was too. Each matched
what Un asked for. But "matches the request" was doing the work that "understands the
request" should have been doing. I never asked what `(Th)` meant. If I had asked that in
the first minute, the whole hardcoded-table branch of this session would not exist, and
Un would not have had to revise me four times to get there.

The part I am glad about is the verification. I did not trust the read-back. I captured a
live payload, checked the derived string against what Un independently reported, and
watched `un` and `kla` write themselves through the real statusline. Two accounts
converged on days Un had named without seeing my code. That is the kind of evidence I
should be producing by default, and here I did.

## Honest Feedback

**1. I offered invented options instead of asking a plain question.** My first
`AskUserQuestion` presented three naming *schemes* I made up. Un rejected it outright to
clarify instead. He already knew what he wanted; my choices were noise dressed as
helpfulness. When the answer lives entirely in the user's head, the honest move is one
open question, not a menu.

**2. I hardcoded before checking whether the data existed.** This is the real failure.
`statusline-command.sh` already parsed `resets_at`, and I had opened that exact file
earlier in the session. Two full implementations were built and discarded. The cost was
maybe fifteen minutes and four rounds of Un's attention -- all avoidable by asking "what
does this label mean?" before building somewhere to put it.

**3. I named a risk and then shipped past it without measuring.** I told Un the new bar
format would be roughly 50% wider and might overflow, then implemented it anyway and let
him discover on his own screen that it was too cramped. `screencapture` was available the
whole time. I did eventually use it -- but only to confirm the revert, never to inform the
decision. Flagging a risk is not the same as testing it, and I treated it as if it were.

## Lessons Learned

- **A label that encodes data is data.** `(Thu/10PM)` was not a name, it was a rendered
  timestamp. Ask what a value means before building a table to hold it -- the answer
  usually names a source, and a derived value cannot go stale.
- **The file you already opened may already hold the next answer.** I grepped
  `statusline-command.sh` for one variable and missed two lines that mattered. Read the
  file, not the match.
- **An acceptance test proves format, not liveness.** `pass claude/token-trio` still
  measures the required 109 characters while the account behind it is cancelled. The
  `~/.zshrc` guard only refuses an *unset* token, so a dead-but-present one walks through.
- **Decoupling beat renaming.** `CLAUDE_TOKEN_NAME` is auth key, filename, and sandbox
  bookmark at once; changing it to alter a label would have broken a security-scoped
  bookmark for cosmetics. Finding what a variable actually does before touching it saved
  the whole thing.
